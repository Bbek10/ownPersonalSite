The backup box only needs to be awake for twenty minutes a night. Leaving it running the other twenty-three hours costs more in electricity than the drives cost in depreciation, so: wake it, back up, put it back to sleep.

Simple in theory. It took three evenings.

The order that matters

Wake-on-LAN is enabled in two places and both have to agree. The firmware setting is the one that counts — if the NIC loses power at shutdown, nothing the operating system says will bring it back. 

# is the adapter armed?
ethtool eno1 | grep -i wake
# Supports Wake-on: pumbg
# Wake-on: g          ← "g" is what you want

Mine reported d — disabled — after every reboot, no matter what I set. The driver was resetting it on link-up, which a one-line systemd unit fixes by re-arming the adapter each boot.

Sending the packet

A magic packet is a broadcast, so it does not cross a subnet boundary on its own. If the sender and the sleeping machine sit on different VLANs — and after splitting the network mine did — the packet has to be directed at the target segment's broadcast address, and the firewall has to allow it. 

wakeonlan -i 10.20.30.255 aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff

Proving it works

The useful test is not "did it wake" but "did it wake from a cold room at 3am after a power cut". Schedule it, then check the logs a week later. Anything that only works when you are watching is not automation.
